The Long Way Round to Lisbon
  
When the ferry finally pulled out of the harbour it was already past
nine, and the light had gone the colour of weak tea. Marguerite stood
at the rail with her coat buttoned wrong and watched the town shrink
into a smear of orange lamps. She had told nobody she was leaving.

"You'll regret it," her sister had said, three weeks earlier, over a
plate of cold potatoes. "Nobody moves to Portugal at fifty-two."

She had not answered. There was nothing to say that would not start
the argument again, and besides, the tickets were already bought.

The crossing took eleven hours. Somewhere around two in the morning
the wind picked up and the lounge emptied; a man in a yellow oilskin
asked whether she minded if he sat, and she said she did not. His name
was Tomás. He had been a fisherman, then a teacher, then a fisherman
again, and he told the story of each change as though it were the only
sensible thing that could have happened.

"People think a life is a line," he said. "Mine is more of a knot."

They talked about bread, about the price of petrol, about whether it
is possible to be homesick for a place you disliked. Tomás said yes,
obviously. He said the Portuguese even have a word for it, saudade,
and that it cannot be translated, which is what people say about every
word they are fond of.

By dawn the sea had flattened. Gulls wheeled over the stern, and the
coast appeared as a thin grey line, then as hills, then as roofs.
Marguerite found that her hands had stopped shaking. She bought two
coffees from the machine near the stairs, one for herself and one for
Tomás, who had fallen asleep with his cap over his eyes.

At the dock a porter shouted something she did not catch. She picked
up her suitcase, the one with the broken wheel, and stepped onto the
quay. It was 7:40 in the morning, 14 degrees, and the whole day was
still hers.
